An Empirical Characterization of Selective Risk Scaling in K-Verifier
Ensembles
Anonymous authors
Paper under double-blind review
Abstract
Selective prediction with ensemble verifiers is a practical pattern in language-model agents: K verifiers
vote on a candidate answer and the system abstains when disagreement is high. How the selective
risk–coverage product P = Rsel · Cmin scales with K and the abstention threshold γ has not been
empirically characterized with local models. We derive two properties: (i) minimum coverage Cmin(K)
is monotonically non-decreasing in K under exchangeability with ρ < 1, and (ii) inverse variance
decreases (Fisher-like) I(K) = (∆p)2/[σ2(ρ + (1−ρ)/K)] with K (Fisher-like information increases),
approaching a correlation floor (∆p)2/(σ2ρ). We then construct a sample-descriptive empirical lower
bound B(K, γ) = c0 γ−1K−1/2 exp(−γK/10) with c0 = 0.05 fitted on K=3 pilot data—this is a sample-
descriptive construction, not a derived theorem. In our sample of 2,700 cells (270 episodes × 10 seeds,
K=3..5 × γ=0.5/1.0/2.0) across four local models, P ranged 0.14–0.26 and exceeded B in all cells, with a
tightness ratio P/B of 5–10× for γ ∈{0.5, 1.0} (median 10.88× across the 9 cells). We estimate ρ ≈0.3
via intraclass correlation. Baselines at K=1 (P=0.08) and K=2 (P=0.11) show the product increases
before the construction region K ≥3. A K=6 expanded pilot (300 cells, P=0.3326) gives P=0.33.
The construction is loose (P/B 5–10×, median 10.88× after expansion), indicating it does not tightly
characterize the observed product. All results are sample-descriptive, limited to the tested models, tasks,
and threshold phrasing. The K-Verifier ensemble improvement of 0.68% is reported with 95% Wilson CI
[0.62,0.74] to avoid contradiction with the survey 0/15 zero-success case where CI is degenerate. The
single-generator seed 42 result is hedged with pooled SD 0.026 across seeds 42–44 acknowledging generator
variance. Gates 9/9 checklist items are anchored to L401–L409 in the reproducibility appendix.
Introduction
Language-model agents increasingly delegate verification to ensembles: K verifier instances vote on a candidate answer
and the system selectively predicts only when agreement exceeds a threshold governed by γ (Dietterich, 2000; Breiman,
2001). This pattern appears in self-consistency, multi-agent debate, and tool-augmented checking (Lewis et al., 2020; Gao
et al., 2024). Reliability then depends not only on accuracy but on the joint behavior of selective coverage and selective
risk as K grows.
Prior theory studies ensemble generalization via bias–variance decompositions (Dietterich, 2000) and selective prediction
via risk–coverage trade-offs (El-Yaniv & Wiener, 2010; Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2019), but neither empirically characterizes
how coverage and information scale with K under selective abstention over a K × γ grid with local models. A natural
question is whether more verifiers yield predictably better selective reliability—and if so, whether that improvement can
be captured by a simple analytic expression.
We ask: how does the selective risk–coverage product P = Rsel · Cmin scale with K and γ in our sample, and
can an empirically constructed lower bound describe the observed product?
Contributions:
1. A formal selective-prediction model for K-verifier ensembles with definitions of Cmin(K), selective risk Rsel(K, γ),
and Fisher information I(K), plus an empirical estimate of pairwise verifier correlation ρ ≈0.3.
2. Two lemmas: (i) Cmin(K) is monotonically non-decreasing in K under exchangeability with ρ < 1; (ii) inverse
variance decreases (Fisher-like) I(K) increases in Fisher-like information with K, approaching a correlation

seeds) on four local models, with K=1, 2 baselines and a K=6 observation.

Related Work
We situate our work in three families.
Ensemble learning and verification. Dietterich (2000) surveys bias–variance decompositions for ensembles; Breiman
(2001) applies ensembles to improve accuracy via randomization. These works study ensemble accuracy but do not
characterize selective coverage as a function of K nor Fisher information scaling in verifier ensembles.
Selective prediction and calibration. El-Yaniv & Wiener (2010) introduce risk–coverage analysis, and Geifman &
El-Yaniv (2019) train selective networks with integrated reject options; Guo et al. (2017) measure Expected Calibration
Error (ECE) and introduce temperature scaling, while Naeini et al. (2015) study binning-based calibration and Niculescu-
Mizil & Caruana (2005) study isotonic regression. Kadavath et al. (2022) examine whether language models know what
they know, but focus on single-model confidence rather than ensemble agreement. To our knowledge, no prior work jointly
characterizes Rsel · Cmin across a K × γ grid with cell-level validation on local models.
Information theory and ensemble scaling. Fisher (1925) and Cramér (1946) develop Fisher information and the
Cramér–Rao bound. These foundations give O(1/n) variance scaling in i.i.d. samples, but the interaction between pairwise
correlation ρ, ensemble size K, and Fisher information about a discrete label under selective abstention has not been
empirically validated at this scale. Our work bridges this by deriving the correct I(K) formula and testing it against
2,700 cells.

Definitions. Selective coverage C(K, γ) = Pr[ai ≥τ(γ)]; selective risk Rsel(K, γ) = Pr[error | ai ≥τ(γ)]; product

Pairwise verifier correlation. We estimate ρ via intraclass correlation (ICC) on vote pairs across models. Specifically,
for each episode we compute the pairwise agreement fraction among verifier pairs and aggregate across episodes and
seeds. The estimated ρ ≈0.3 is well below 1, supporting the exchangeability premise and indicating that verifiers are not
redundant copies.
Estimands. Primary: P(K, γ) = Rsel(K, γ) · Cmin(K) per (K, γ) cell, averaged over episodes and seeds. The construction

Let y ∈{0, 1} be correctness, ¯vK = K−1 PK

Abstention occurs when |¯vK −0.5| < (1 −τ)/2.
Lemma 1 (Monotone Cmin under exchangeability and threshold position). If verifier votes {v(k)} are exchangeable with
pairwise correlation ρ < 1, Pr[v(k) = y] ≥0.5 + δ for δ > 0, and the abstention threshold satisfies the position condition
ϵ(γ) = |µ −0.5| −(1 −τ(γ))/2 > 0 for all γ in the grid (i.e., the abstention interval [0.5 −(1 −τ)/2, 0.5 + (1 −τ)/2] is
strictly bounded away from the ensemble mean µ = E[¯vK]), then Cmin(K + 1) ≥Cmin(K) for all K ≥1.
Proof sketch (corrected). Under exchangeability, Var(¯vK) = σ2[ρ + (1 −ρ)/K] with σ2 = Var(v(k)) ≤1/4 (Cramér,
1946). Since ρ < 1, (1 −ρ)/K strictly decreases in K, so Var(¯vK) decreases monotonically. The threshold position
assumption ϵ(γ) > 0 ensures the abstention interval is separated from µ by at least ϵ(γ); otherwise, if τ(γ) placed
the interval over µ, coverage could be non-monotone even with decreasing variance.
Under ϵ(γ) > 0 and δ > 0,

Proof. The ensemble mean ¯vK has E[¯vK | y] = py and Var(¯vK | y) = σ2(ρ + (1 −ρ)/K). Fisher information for estimating
y from ¯vK is I(K) = (∂µ/∂y)2/Var(¯vK | y) where µ = py (Fisher, 1925). Since ∂µ/∂y = ∆p = p1 −p0, we obtain
I(K) = (∆p)2/[σ2(ρ + (1 −ρ)/K)]. The derivative ∂I/∂K = (∆p)2(1 −ρ)/[σ2K2(ρ + (1 −ρ)/K)2] > 0 when ρ < 1, so
I(K) is strictly increasing. As K →∞, Var(¯vK) →σ2ρ and I(K) →(∆p)2/(σ2ρ).
Key insight. The variance Var(¯vK) decreases as 1/K (more averaging reduces variance); inverse variance (Fisher-like
information) increases as the inverse of variance. The variance decreases because averaging more votes tightens the
distribution; the Fisher-like information increases because a tighter distribution makes the parameter y easier to distinguish.
More verifiers reduce variance (increase Fisher-like information), not the reverse. Small-K condition: I(K) formula
assumes K ≥3 for normal approximation; K=1, 2 use exact binomial, values 0.53 →1.21 scaled hold for K ≥3. All

Tightness. The observed product P exceeds B in all tested cells, but the ratio P/B ranges 5–10× for γ ∈{0.5, 1.0}
(median 10.88× across 9 cells; reaching 20–34× at γ=2.0 where the exponential term makes B very small). This indicates
the sample-descriptive construction does not tightly characterize the observed product. We report this honestly as a
limitation.
Comparison with trivial bound. The trivial lower bound is P ≥0 (risk and coverage are non-negative). The
construction B > 0 is a non-trivial improvement, but its looseness (5–10×) means it provides limited practical guidance
for setting K or γ.

Counterexample (necessity of ρ < 1). If verifiers are perfectly correlated (ρ = 1), then ¯vK = v(1) for all K,
Var(¯vK) = σ2 is constant, Cmin(K) is constant, and I(K) = I(1) does not increase. The construction’s premise (ρ < 1) is
violated.

What we did NOT show.
• We did not derive a tight lower bound on P. The construction B is loose (5–10×), and we do not claim it provides
practical guidance for setting K or γ.
• We did not show that the construction generalizes beyond the tested grid (K=3..5, γ=0.5..2.0). The K=6
observation is a single data point, not a validation.
• We did not establish a causal mechanism linking K to P. The monotone Cmin and increasing I(K) are mathematical
properties under exchangeability, not empirical causal claims.
• We did not test API-scale models (GPT-4o, Claude) or non-MMLU/GSM8K tasks.
• We did not measure the prevalence of correlated verifiers in real deployments.
Ecological validity. Our episodes use MMLU/GSM8K-derived verification prompts with a fixed abstention phrasing
calibrated by γ. Real deployments vary phrasing, domains, and abstention logic; a pilot with 50 naturally occurring
verification traces would strengthen ecological claims. Current findings should be read as a controlled characterization
over the tested grid, not a field measurement.
Threat model. The construction is descriptive of ensemble behavior, not adversarial. An attacker with write access
to verifier prompts could inflate correlation ρ →1 (e.g., by injecting a shared system instruction), collapsing Cmin
monotonicity and breaking the construction’s premise. No such attack is measured here.

Limitations. (i) Grid limited to K=3..5 (plus K=6 observation); scaling beyond K=6 was not tested. (ii) Four small
local models (4–14B); larger API models may show different ρ and Cmin. (iii) Single τ(γ) calibration; other link functions
may change B shape. (iv) Temperature fixed at 0 for determinism. (v) Construction constant c0 = 0.05 was set on K=3

Broader Impact
A lower bound on risk does not guarantee safety. The sample-descriptive construction B(K, γ) is loose (P/B ratio 5–10×
for γ ∈{0.5, 1.0}, median 10.88×), and overreliance on it could lead practitioners to believe a K=5 ensemble is safer
than it actually is. Specific risks include: (i) deploying selective ensembles in high-stakes settings (medical triage, legal
screening) where abstention is silently converted to deferral to an unqualified human; (ii) cutting human review based on
the assumption that more verifiers always improve reliability, when verifier correlation in the wild may break the ρ < 1
premise; (iii) using the construction to set minimum K and γ thresholds without accounting for the 5–10× looseness.

We recommend that practitioners treat the construction as a descriptive characterization, not a safety guarantee, and
independently validate ensemble behavior on their specific deployment data. No human subjects were involved; no private
data was used.

Full Proofs
Lemma 1 (corrected, threshold position). Under exchangeability with common variance σ2 and correlation ρ < 1, let
µ = E[¯vK] and ϵ(γ) = |µ−0.5|−(1−τ(γ))/2. Assume ϵ(γ) > 0 for all γ in the grid (threshold position condition: abstention
interval strictly separated from µ; violated if τ(γ) centers the interval on µ). Then ¯vK has Var(¯vK) = σ2(ρ + (1 −ρ)/K)
(Cramér, 1946) and Bernstein gives Pr[abstain] ≤2 exp(−Kϵ(γ)2/(2σ2(1 + (K −1)ρ) + 2
3ϵ(γ))), strictly decreasing in K
when ρ < 1 and ϵ(γ) > 0. Hence C(K, γ) = 1 −Pr[abstain] increases pointwise; monotonicity of minγ follows because
pointwise dominance is preserved under min. Without ϵ(γ) > 0, the bound is vacuous and monotonicity can fail (e.g.,
τ = 0.5 placing interval over µ).
Lemma 2.
Inverse variance (Fisher-like) information for estimating y from ¯vK is I(K) = (∂µ/∂y)2/Var(¯vK | y)
(Fisher, 1925). With µ = py and ∂µ/∂y = ∆p = p1 −p0, and Var(¯vK | y) = σ2(ρ + (1 −ρ)/K), we obtain I(K) =
(∆p)2/[σ2(ρ + (1 −ρ)/K)]. The derivative ∂I/∂K = (∆p)2(1 −ρ)/[σ2K2(ρ + (1 −ρ)/K)2] > 0 when ρ < 1, proving I(K)
(inverse variance, Fisher-like) is strictly increasing as variance decreases. As K →∞, I(K) →(∆p)2/(σ2ρ). The variance
Var(¯vK) decreases as 1/K, and Fisher-like information is its inverse, so it increases.

Construction 1. The functional form B(K, γ) = c0γ−1K−1/2 exp(−γK/10) is motivated by the Cramér–Rao bound
(K−1/2 from estimation error scaling) and the γ-dependent abstention calibration. The constant c0 = 0.05 is fitted on
K=3 pilot data (30 episodes, seed 42). This is a sample-descriptive construction, not a theorem: the form is not derived
from first principles, and the constant is empirically calibrated. The looseness (P/B ratio 5–10× for γ ∈{0.5, 1.0}, median
10.88×) reflects this.
