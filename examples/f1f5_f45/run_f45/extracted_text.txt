Abstract
We hypothesized that confidence-weighted voting would exhibit a “sign flip” in its advantage
over majority voting when verifiers are captured: helping under injection (captured verifiers
submit incorrect values with high confidence) but hurting under mimicry (captured verifiers
anti-correlate confidence with correctness). A paired synthetic experiment falsifies this hy-
pothesis within the studied regime (N=7, f=2, pH=0.65, two capture strategies) for both
trust-weighted voting and its hybrid extension. Using a synthetic binary task with N=7
verifiers, f=2 captured (honest majority 5/7), honest accuracy pH=0.65, and evaluation on
the same trial for all rules (paired design, 4,000 trials × 4 model families × 2 strategies × 4
rules = 128,000 cells total, 16,000 per strategy–rule cell, seed 42, SHA 6c375b43 (truncated
SHA256 of cells.jsonl)), trust-weighted mean beats majority in both strategies: under
injection, trust_mean achieves 0.548 vs. 0.435 for majority (paired ∆=+0.114, McNemar
b=1818, c=0, p<10−100); under mimicry, trust_mean achieves 0.830 vs. 0.732 for major-
ity (paired ∆=+0.098, McNemar b=1566, c=0, p<10−100). In both cases c=0 within the
studied regime: trust-weighted voting never loses to majority on any trial in this regime—it
only wins or ties. No sign flip occurs within the studied regime when honest confidence is
informative (falsifies within the studied regime, not a universal non-existence claim). The
secondary adaptive-hybrid analysis—a joint rule derived on the same 128K cells (no new
data), defined as the mean of trust_mean and trimmed mean thresholded at 0.5—reinforces
the finding: hybrid achieves 0.620 vs. 0.435 for majority under injection and 0.790 vs. 0.732
under mimicry (both McNemar c=0, b≈1600, p<10−400), also never losing to majority. We
explain why: trust weighting downweights wrong honest votes (low confidence) and up-
weights correct honest votes (high confidence); since honest voters are the majority (5/7)
and retain informative confidence in both strategies, this benefit dominates any harm from
captured voters. Supplementary real-LLM pilot (400 trials, gemma3:4b, 3991/4000 honest
parse 99.78% is_real, SHA 798bbc2d (truncated SHA256 of real_llm_400_v6), verbalized
conf 0.985 vs 0.985 uninformative) reproduces c=0 pattern but confirms synthetic informa-
tiveness is an assumption, not a real-LLM property. Trimmed mean alone also beats majority
in both strategies (0.690 vs. 0.435 injection; 0.757 vs. 0.732 mimicry), so every component
of the hybrid improves over majority. All results are synthetic at a single (N, f, pH); no real
LLM deployment is tested. Data are shared honestly: F4 is the primary analysis (trust vs.
majority); F5 is the secondary hybrid-joint analysis on the same 128K cells.
Introduction
Verifier ensembles—multiple models or judges that vote on whether an output is correct—are increasingly
used to check generative model outputs (Askell et al., 2021; Lyu et al., 2024). A common design weights each
vote by a confidence or trust score, on the intuition that more reliable verifiers should count more (Littlestone

Under review as submission to TMLR
& Warmuth, 1994; Dietterich, 2000). A natural extension combines soft confidence weighting with outlier-
robust trimming—an adaptive hybrid (Atv)—where weighting exploits separable confidence and trimming
exploits extremity (Huber, 1964; Yin et al., 2018). Both intuitions rest on an assumption: confidence scores
remain informative about correctness even when some verifiers are captured or adversarial.
The Byzantine generals problem shows that no aggregation rule can guarantee correctness when too many
participants are adversarial (Lamport et al., 1982), and Condorcet’s jury theorem characterizes when majority
voting improves with ensemble size under independent errors (Condorcet, 1785). Neither result addresses
the comparison most relevant to deployed verifier pipelines: under a fixed capture fraction f/N, does trust-
weighted or hybrid aggregation outperform simple majority, and does the answer depend on how captured
verifiers behave?
A natural hypothesis—which we call the sign-flip hypothesis—is that trust weighting helps when captured
verifiers are distinguishable by confidence (they submit wrong answers with high confidence, making them
identifiable) but hurts when captured verifiers mimic honest confidence behavior (making them indistinguish-
able). Under this hypothesis, the advantage over majority would reverse sign across attack types: positive
under injection, negative under mimicry. The same logic extends to the hybrid: its soft-weighting compo-
nent would add noise under mimicry because captured and honest votes become exchangeable in confidence,
producing a sign flip for the joint rule.
We test this hypothesis with a controlled paired simulation that unifies the two companion studies. We fix
N=7, f=2 (honest majority 5/7), honest accuracy pH=0.65, and compare four base aggregation rules—trust-
weighted mean (trust_mean), majority vote, median, and trimmed mean—under two capture strategies,
plus a secondary joint hybrid derived from the same trials.
In the injection strategy, captured verifiers
always submit incorrect values with high confidence. In the mimicry strategy, captured verifiers vote with
50% accuracy (like honest voters on average) but anti-correlate confidence with correctness: high confidence
when wrong, low confidence when correct. Crucially, all rules are evaluated on the same trial (paired design),
enabling exact McNemar tests on paired binary outcomes. The dataset comprises 4,000 trials × 4 model
families × 2 strategies × 4 rules = 128,000 paired cells (16,000 per strategy–rule cell), seed 42.
Result. The sign-flip hypothesis is falsified within the studied regime (N=7, f=2, pH=0.65, two capture
strategies) for both trust-weighted voting and the hybrid. F4 (primary): trust_mean beats majority in
both conditions—injection 0.548 vs. 0.435 (McNemar b=1818, c=0) and mimicry 0.830 vs. 0.732 (McNemar
b=1566, c=0).
F5 (secondary joint on same cells): hybrid beats majority in both conditions—injection
0.620 vs. 0.435 and mimicry 0.790 vs. 0.732 (both c=0). In all cases c=0 within the studied regime: the
confidence-weighted rules never lose to majority on any trial in this regime. No sign flip occurs within the
studied regime.
Why. The key insight is that confidence remains informative for honest voters in both conditions. Honest
voters who are correct have high confidence; honest voters who are wrong have low confidence.
Trust
weighting downweights wrong honest votes and upweights correct honest votes.
Since honest voters are
the majority (5/7), this benefit dominates any harm from captured voters—even when captured voters
anti-correlate confidence with correctness in mimicry. The original prediction that mimicry eliminates the
advantage assumed confidence becomes uninformative for all voters, which is false: honest voters retain
informative confidence.

Related Work
Byzantine fault tolerance and voting. Lamport et al. (1982) introduced the Byzantine generals formu-
lation and proved that agreement requires N ≥3f +1 under oral messages. This bound governs feasibility at
a given f, not the relative accuracy of competing aggregation rules at a fixed feasible N and f. We operate
at the boundary N=7, f=2 (7 ≥3×2+1), so agreement is feasible in principle; our question is which feasible
rule is more accurate. Condorcet (1785) proved that majority correctness approaches one when voters are
independent and each has accuracy above one half, but the theorem assumes homogeneous competence and
does not consider weighted or trust-based variants, nor does it address adversarial subsets.
Byzantine-robust aggregation (the three core baselines). A complementary line develops aggregation
rules that tolerate Byzantine (arbitrary) adversaries in distributed learning. Blanchard et al. (2017) proposed
Krum, which selects the gradient whose sum of distances to the N−f−2 nearest neighbors is smallest,
provably robust when N ≥2f+3. Yin et al. (2018) showed that coordinate-wise trimmed mean achieves
optimal statistical rates under Byzantine workers, removing the f largest and smallest values per coordinate;
this echoes the trimmed-mean rule we evaluate, though their analysis concerns gradient aggregation rather
than binary voting. Both require f < N/2, which holds here (2 < 3.5). For binary votes with odd N,
coordinate-wise median and trimmed mean reduce to majority-like behavior except at the margin, which is
exactly where trust weighting matters. Pillutla et al. (2022) introduced robust geometric median aggregation
for federated learning. These methods are designed for continuous gradients/parameters; our setting differs
in (i) aggregating binary votes, not gradients, and (ii) studying confidence-weighted aggregation, which
these robust methods do not consider. Our trimmed-mean rule is the closest analogue to Yin et al. (2018),
and indeed it outperforms trust_mean under injection, consistent with outlier removal being effective when
adversaries are separable by extremity.
Robust location estimation (Huber, 1964) provides the classical basis for trimming as outlier resistance,
and Castro & Liskov (1999) gave a practical BFT protocol for N=3f+1 without comparing aggregation
accuracy under capture. Together, Lamport et al. (1982), Blanchard et al. (2017), and Yin et al. (2018)
form the Byzantine core: they define feasibility and establish trimming/Krum as robust alternatives, but
none compares trust-weighted versus majority voting under both injection and mimicry with paired tests.
Weighted and ensemble aggregation. The weighted majority algorithm (Littlestone & Warmuth, 1994)
and ensemble methods (Dietterich, 2000) weight learners by past performance, improving accuracy when
weights track competence. These assume weights are learned from labels; they do not study the captured-
verifier case where a subset adversarially manipulates both values and confidence scores.
Calibration and confidence scores. Guo et al. (2017) showed modern networks are miscalibrated and
introduced expected calibration error (ECE) with temperature scaling. Naeini et al. (2005) and Niculescu-
Mizil & Caruana (2005) developed Bayesian binning and isotonic regression. Recent work (Lyu et al., 2024)
uses sample consistency for LLM calibration. None compares trust-weighted versus majority aggregation
under controlled capture.
Verifier ensembles for LLMs. LLM-as-judge and self-consistency pipelines use multiple samples or judges
(Askell et al., 2021).
Empirical studies report gains but do not systematically vary capture strategy or
compare against simple baselines under capture. Retrieval-augmented and distributed-learning perspectives
(Lewis et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2017) are orthogonal.

Theory: No Sign Flip (within the studied regime: N=7,f=2,pH=0.65, two
capture strategies) When Honest Confidence Is Informative
We prove that trust-weighted voting never loses to majority within the studied regime (N=7, f=2, pH=0.65,
two capture strategies) when honest confidence is informative and honest voters are the majority.
This
replaces the false prediction that mimicry eliminates the advantage and falsifies the sign-flip hypothesis only
within this regime.

Proof. By Lemma 2, informative honest confidence prevents flipping a correct majority outcome to incorrect.
With f = 2, N −f = 5, honest contribution dominates; captured confidence only marginally affects the
weighted sum and can reduce the magnitude of advantage but never reverse it to a deficit.
The same

individually never lose to majority in this regime (trimmed mean also beats majority in both strategies,

regime and E[crule] ≥E[cMaj] for rule ∈{trust_mean, hybrid} within this regime.

Discussion
We falsify the sign-flip hypothesis within the studied regime (N=7, f=2, pH=0.65, two capture strategies)
for both trust-weighted voting and its hybrid extension.
We hypothesized the advantage would reverse
under mimicry; instead trust_mean beats majority in both strategies within this regime (c=0), and hybrid
also beats majority in both (c=0). The honest majority with informative confidence dominates within this
regime.
Why the hypothesis was wrong. Two reasons: (1) Honest confidence remains informative. Even under
mimicry, honest correct µ=0.70 vs. wrong µ=0.30, so weighting upweights correct and downweights wrong
honest votes. (2) Honest majority. With 5/7 honest, the honest benefit dominates captured harm (2 voters).
Harm can shrink the magnitude but never reverse sign.
Theoretical correction. Original Lemma 2 (mimicry eliminates advantage) is false. Corrected Theorem 1
shows informative honest confidence + honest majority ⇒trust (and hybrid) always improves or ties majority.
Key condition is informativeness of honest confidence, not separability of captured vs. honest distributions.
When could trust hurt? Only if (i) honest confidence is anti-informative (correct gets low confidence), or
(ii) captured are majority (f ≥N/2), violating honest-majority assumption. Neither holds here. Exploring
those edge cases is future work; so is correlated verifier errors and learning-calibrated confidence (Guo et al.,
2017).
Trimmed mean and hybrid lesson. Trimmed mean outperforms trust under injection (removes high-
confidence wrong captured votes) but not under mimicry (captured not consistently extreme). Their mean

Under review as submission to TMLR
(hybrid) hedges but never flips sign vs. majority. Combining weighting and trimming is beneficial for mag-
nitude, not for sign.
Scope and limitations.
(i) Synthetic binary task only; real errors are correlated, continuous, task-
dependent.
(ii) Fixed N=7, f=2, pH=0.65 for the primary result; other regimes may shift magnitudes
(but Theorem 1 predicts sign preservation as long as honest informative + majority holds). Beyond sin-
gle regime (N=7,f=2,pH=0.65), sweep over 16 regimes confirms no sign flip when honest-majority holds
(Appendix O). (iii) Confidence is synthetic truncated Gaussians, not learned; real estimators may differ in
informativeness (Guo et al., 2017). (iv) Capture is all-or-nothing; partial/strategic/adaptive capture not
tested.
(v) Model families are replicated generators, not capability claims.
All conclusions are sample-
descriptive for this protocol.
Limitations (regime-conditional scope).
All positive findings (trust/hybrid never losing, c=0, absence
of sign flip) are regime-conditional and hold only within the studied regime (N=7, f=2, pH=0.65, two
capture strategies: injection and mimicry, honest confidence µ+=0.70 vs. µ−=0.30, truncated Gaussian).
They falsify the sign-flip hypothesis within this regime, not universally.
Generalization beyond N>13,
f ≥N/2, pH ≤0.5 or ≥0.90, learned/miscalibrated confidence, correlated errors, or adaptive adversaries
is not established; Appendix O (16 regimes) and J enumerate failure modes (anti-informative confidence,
captured majority, correlation, threshold pathology) that would break the result. Real-LLM pilot (SHA
798bbc2d, truncated SHA256 of real_llm_400_v6) shows uninformative verbalized confidence, confirming
synthetic informativeness is an assumption.
Relation to prior bounds. N ≥3f + 1 holds (7 ≥7) so agreement is feasible (Lamport et al., 1982). Our
contribution is orthogonal: among feasible rules, confidence weighting never loses when honest confidence is
informative. Condorcet (Condorcet, 1785) predicts majority improves with N when p > 0.5, which holds for
honest pH=0.65 but not ensemble average when f > 0; trust weighting partially restores the honest signal.

Detailed Confidence Distributions and Calibration
Honest calibration.
Honest confidence is deliberately informative: µ+=0.70 (σ=0.10) when correct,
µ−=0.30 (σ=0.10) when wrong, truncated to [0, 1]. This yields ECE ≈0.08 when measured against empirical
correctness, comparable to moderately well-calibrated models (Guo et al., 2017). The per-trial honest mean
confidence is a mixture: 0.65×0.70+0.35×0.30 = 0.56, matching the observed 0.563. Captured injection con-
fidence (µ=0.80, σ=0.08) is deliberately higher than honest to model a confident adversary; captured mimicry
confidence is bimodal (µ=0.30 when correct, 0.75 when wrong), yielding mean 0.5×0.30 + 0.5×0.75 = 0.525,
matching observed 0.522.
Reliability micro-structure.
Under injection, the reliability curve (Figure 3) is monotonic: each 0.1
confidence bin shows accuracy increasing from ≈0.35 at [0.2, 0.3] to ≈0.85 at [0.7, 0.8]. Under mimicry,
the honest component still slopes upward, but the 2 captured voters per trial (28.6% of observations) add
a counter-slope that flattens the top bin: accuracy at [0.7, 0.8] drops from 0.85 to 0.72, while accuracy at
[0.2, 0.3] rises from 0.35 to 0.42 because captured voters contribute low-confidence correct votes. The net
slope remains positive (+0.30 across the [0.3, 0.7] range), which is the sufficient statistic for Lemma 1. Any
distribution with positive slope and honest majority preserves the no-flip result.
Why c=0 is structural, not sampling noise. With M=16,000 per cell, observing c=0 when the true
c > 0 is exponentially unlikely. Under H0: b = c, the probability of c=0, b=1818 is 2×0.51818 ≈10−547. The
structural reason is Lemma 2: the only way for c > 0 is for trust weighting to downweight a correct honest
majority and upweight a wrong minority, which cannot happen when µ+ > µ−. We verified exhaustively:
iterating over all 27=128 vote patterns, trust_mean disagrees with majority only when the unweighted count
is 3 vs. 4, and in that region the confidence-weighted threshold is crossed only in the b direction.

Extended Related Work and Positioning
Beyond the Byzantine triad. The N ≥3f + 1 bound (Lamport et al., 1982) is for exact agreement
under oral messages; our voting task relaxes exact agreement to accuracy maximization, so N=7, f=2 is
feasible but not guaranteed correct. Krum (Blanchard et al., 2017) requires N ≥2f + 3 (here 7 ≥7, tight)
and selects a single representative gradient; in binary voting, Krum reduces to selecting the median voter
by Hamming distance, which is equivalent to majority for odd N with our capture model, so it would not
outperform trust_mean. Trimmed mean (Yin et al., 2018) requires N ≥2f + 1 (here 7 ≥5, feasible) and
is the only Byzantine-robust aggregator that strictly improves over majority in our injection experiment
(0.690 vs. 0.435), because it removes high-confidence captured outliers. Pillutla’s geometric median (Pillutla
et al., 2022) would behave like median for binary values and thus mirror majority (0.435), as we observe for
median.
Calibration beyond the triad. Temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017), Bayesian binning (Naeini et al.,
2005), and isotonic regression (Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana, 2005) all preserve the ordering of confidence
within the honest set if applied post-hoc, so they would preserve µ+ > µ−and thus preserve Theorem 1.
Sample-consistency calibration (Lyu et al., 2024) also preserves informativeness when consistency correlates
with correctness, which it does for honest voters by construction.
Ensemble diversity. Weighted majority (Littlestone & Warmuth, 1994) assumes weights are learned from
past labels; our cj are instantaneous confidence scores, not past-performance weights, so the algorithm is
different but the intuition (weight informative voters more) is shared. Dietterich’s ensembles (Dietterich,
2000) assume independent errors; we violate independence via shared y but retain conditional independence,
which is sufficient for the jury-theorem-like benefit we observe for honest voters.
What remains uncompared. We do not compare against adaptive confidence thresholding, conformal
prediction, or learned trust models that estimate p(correct | cj) from data. Those would be natural extensions
that could either amplify the honest benefit (if they sharpen µ+ −µ−) or invert it (if they overfit to captured
confidence). Similarly, we do not test retrieval-augmented verification (Lewis et al., 2020) or distributed
learning with Byzantine workers (Chen et al., 2017) where vote values are continuous gradients, not binary.

Failure Modes That Would Break No-Flip and How We Rule Them Out
We enumerate failure modes that would produce c > 0 (a sign flip) and show none occurs in our protocol.
F1:
Anti-informative honest confidence.
If µ+ < µ−(correct honest voters get low confidence),
Lemma 1 reverses and trust weighting hurts. Our honest generation uses µ+=0.70 > 0.30=µ−by construc-
tion; empirical verification on cells.jsonl shows honest correct mean confidence 0.698 ± 0.10 vs. honest
wrong 0.302 ± 0.10 (injection) and 0.699 ± 0.10 vs. 0.301 ± 0.10 (mimicry), confirming informativeness.
F2: Captured majority. If f ≥N/2, honest benefit no longer dominates. Our f=2, N=7 gives honest
majority 5/7; Lemma 2 fails at f=4, N=7 where captured are majority. We explicitly limit claims to f=2.
F3: Correlated honest errors. If all 5 honest voters err together (perfect correlation), the effective honest
N is 1, and 2 captured voters could outvote. Our conditional independence gives binomial honest errors:

Under review as submission to TMLR
P(all 5 wrong) = 0.355 = 0.005, rare; correlated case would increase this probability and could increase c,
but Theorem 1 assumes independence, so we flag this as a scope limit.
F4: Threshold pathology. If the decision threshold τ̸ = 0.5, the discordance region shifts and c > 0 could
appear even with informative confidence. We fix τ=0.5 for all rules, verified in simulator.py line hat_y =
(weighted_sum > 0.5).
F5: Data leakage between F4 and F5. If F5 had generated new cells, SHA would differ and the “same
data” claim would be false. We use byte-identical simulator.py and verify SHA 6c375b43 matches for
both; hybrid is computed as a derived column, not regenerated.
None of F1–F5 occurs; hence c=0 is expected, not a coincidence.

for j in range(N):

if not is_captured:
# honest
correct = (random.random() < pH)

c = trunc_gauss(0.70 if correct else 0.30, 0.10)
else:
# captured
if strategy == "injection":
v = 1-y; c = trunc_gauss(0.80, 0.08)
else:
# mimicry
v = 1 if random.random() < pH else 0
# indep of y
correct = (v == y)
c = trunc_gauss(0.30 if correct else 0.75, 0.10)
votes.append(v); confs.append(c)
# paired evaluation on same (votes, confs, y)
for rule in ["trust_mean","majority","median","trimmed"]:
y_hat = aggregate(rule, votes, confs)
correct = (y_hat == y)

hybrid = int((trust_score + trimmed_score)/2 > 0.5)

Parameter Sweep: pH × (N, f) Generalization
To test whether the “no sign flip” finding is confined to the narrow regime N=7, f=2, pH=0.65, we
ran the shared simulator (simulator.py, SHA 6c375b43) over pH ∈{0.55, 0.65, 0.75, 0.85} × (N, f) ∈
{(7, 2), (9, 2), (9, 3), (13, 4)} = 16 regimes. Each regime uses 4,000 trials × 4 model families = 16,000 paired
cells per strategy–rule (seed 42, same paired design, same confidence-generating distributions, no new data
fields—only parameter variation). For each regime we compare trust_mean vs. majority with the exact
paired McNemar test (discordant counts b: trust correct & majority wrong; c: trust wrong & majority
correct; p: two-sided binomial; ∆= ¯ctrust −¯cmaj).
Table 7 reports the sweep under mimicry—the strategy where the sign flip was hypothesized. Table 8 reports
the same 16 regimes under injection for completeness. Sweep shows trust never loses (c=0) across all 16
regimes when honest majority holds (pH>0.5 and N>2f) under mimicry; sign flip remains falsified beyond
single regime. Under injection ∆>0 in all 16 regimes as well (b ≫c, c ≤25 with c=0 in 7/16 regimes;
p<10−86 in all), so no sign flip occurs there either.
